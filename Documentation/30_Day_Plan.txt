ABOUT THIS GUIDE
================================================================================

This 30-day plan is designed to take you from absolute zero to building a fully
functional obstacle-avoiding robot. No prior electronics or programming
experience is required. Each day builds on the previous one, creating a solid
foundation of skills that compound over time.

What makes this guide different:
  • Every day has a THEORY section (what you need to understand)
  • Every day has a PRACTICAL section (what you physically build)
  • Every day has a CHECKPOINT (how you know you succeeded)
  • Troubleshooting tips are included for common mistakes
  • Real-world context explains WHY each concept matters for robotics

Time Commitment: ~1-2 hours per day
Total Cost: ~$50-80 for all components (shopping list provided separately)
Difficulty: Absolute beginner → Intermediate builder

REQUIRED MATERIALS (Overview)
================================================================================

Before starting, gather these essentials:
  • Breadboard (full-size, 830 tie points recommended)
  • Jumper wires (male-to-male, male-to-female, at least 30 of each)
  • Arduino Uno R3 (or compatible clone)
  • USB-A to USB-B cable (for Arduino)
  • LEDs (assorted colors, at least 10)
  • Resistors (220Ω, 330Ω, 1kΩ, 10kΩ — at least 5 of each)
  • Capacitors (100μF electrolytic, 0.1μF ceramic — at least 3 of each)
  • Diodes (1N4007 — at least 3)
  • NPN Transistor (2N2222 or BC547 — at least 3)
  • Potentiometer (10kΩ — at least 2)
  • Push buttons (at least 3)
  • DC motors (3-6V — at least 2)
  • L298N motor driver module
  • HC-SR04 ultrasonic sensor
  • 9V battery + battery clip (or 4xAA holder)
  • Robot chassis kit (2WD with wheels)
  • Multimeter (basic digital)
  • Wire strippers (optional but helpful)

See the separate Shopping List document for links and pricing.

📅 WEEK 1: ELECTRICAL FOUNDATIONS (Days 1–7)
================================================================================

The first week is entirely about understanding electricity and basic components.
You will NOT touch a microcontroller yet — and that's intentional. Many beginners
rush to Arduino and then struggle because they don't understand the underlying
electrical principles. By the end of this week, you'll be able to wire circuits
confidently and debug them when things go wrong.


--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
DAY 1: WHAT IS ELECTRICITY?
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

🎯 LEARNING OBJECTIVES:
  • Understand voltage, current, and resistance
  • Learn Ohm's Law and apply it to a real circuit
  • Light your first LED with proper current limiting

📖 THEORY:

Electricity is the flow of electrons through a conductor. To understand it,
you need three fundamental concepts:

1. VOLTAGE (V) — Measured in Volts
   Think of voltage as "electrical pressure." It's the force that pushes
   electrons through a circuit. A 9V battery has more "push" than a 1.5V
   battery. Voltage is always measured BETWEEN two points — it's a difference
   in electrical potential.

   Real-world analogy: Voltage is like water pressure in a pipe. Higher
   pressure means water flows faster and harder.

2. CURRENT (I) — Measured in Amperes (Amps)
   Current is the actual flow of electrons — how many electrons pass a point
   per second. In robotics, most of our circuits use milliamps (mA), which
   is 1/1000 of an amp.

   Real-world analogy: Current is like the volume of water flowing through
   the pipe. You can have high pressure (voltage) but low flow (current)
   if the pipe is narrow.

3. RESISTANCE (R) — Measured in Ohms (Ω)
   Resistance opposes the flow of current. Every component has some
   resistance. Resistors are components specifically designed to provide
   a precise amount of resistance.

   Real-world analogy: Resistance is like the narrowness of the pipe.
   A narrow pipe restricts water flow even under high pressure.

OHM'S LAW — THE MOST IMPORTANT FORMULA IN ELECTRONICS:

   V = I × R

   This can be rearranged:
   • I = V / R  (to find current)
   • R = V / I  (to find resistance)

   Example: You have a 5V supply and want 20mA through an LED.
   The LED itself drops about 2V, leaving 3V across the resistor.
   R = V / I = 3V / 0.020A = 150Ω
   So you need at least a 150Ω resistor (220Ω is commonly used for safety).

WHY THIS MATTERS FOR ROBOTICS:
   Every motor, sensor, and microcontroller in your robot operates within
   specific voltage and current ranges. Exceed them, and you destroy
   components. Ohm's Law lets you calculate the right resistor values,
   predict current draw, and design safe circuits.

🔨 PRACTICAL: LIGHT AN LED WITH A RESISTOR

Materials needed:
  • 1 breadboard
  • 1 LED (any color)
  • 1 resistor (220Ω — red, red, brown, gold bands)
  • 2 jumper wires
  • 1 battery (9V with clip, or 4xAA pack giving ~6V)

Understanding the breadboard:
  A breadboard is a reusable platform for building circuits without soldering.
  • The outer columns (marked + and −) are POWER RAILS — all holes in each
    rail are connected vertically
  • The inner rows are connected HORIZONTALLY in groups of 5
  • There is a gap in the middle that separates left and right sections

Step-by-step build:
  1. Insert the LED into the breadboard. Note: the LONGER leg is positive
     (anode) and the SHORTER leg is negative (cathode). There's also a flat
     edge on the negative side of the LED casing.

  2. Connect one end of the 220Ω resistor to the SAME row as the LED's
     positive leg (anode).

  3. Connect a jumper wire from the OTHER end of the resistor to the
     positive (+) power rail.

  4. Connect a jumper wire from the LED's negative leg (cathode) to the
     negative (−) power rail.

  5. Connect your battery: positive terminal to the + rail, negative
     terminal to the − rail.

  6. The LED should light up!

What to observe:
  • The LED lights up — current is flowing through the circuit
  • The resistor may feel slightly warm — it's converting excess energy
    to heat (this is normal and expected)

What to try:
  • Reverse the LED (swap the legs) — it should NOT light up because LEDs
    only allow current in one direction
  • Remove the resistor and connect directly — DON'T actually do this with
    high voltage, but with a small coin cell (3V) you can briefly see the
    LED is much brighter. With a 9V battery and no resistor, the LED will
    burn out almost instantly.

⚠️ TROUBLESHOOTING:
  • LED doesn't light: Check polarity (long leg = positive). Make sure
    components are in the SAME row on the breadboard.
  • LED is very dim: Battery might be dead. Try a fresh one.
  • LED burned out: You probably forgot the resistor or used too low a value.

✅ DAY 1 CHECKPOINT:
  □ Can you explain voltage, current, and resistance in your own words?
  □ Can you use Ohm's Law to calculate the resistor needed for an LED?
  □ Did your LED circuit work?
  □ Do you understand why the resistor is needed?

If you answered yes to all four — you're ready for Day 2.

DAY 2: USING TOOLS — THE MULTIMETER
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

🎯 LEARNING OBJECTIVES:
  • Understand what a multimeter measures and how to use it
  • Measure voltage across a battery and components
  • Measure resistance of resistors
  • Learn continuity testing for debugging

📖 THEORY:

A multimeter is the single most important debugging tool in electronics. It
measures three things (hence "multi"):

1. VOLTAGE (V) — DC Voltage mode
   • Set the dial to "V" with a straight line (DC) or "V" with a wavy
     line (AC — we won't use this for robotics)
   • Place the RED probe on the higher-voltage point
   • Place the BLACK probe on the lower-voltage point (ground)
   • The display shows the voltage difference

2. RESISTANCE (Ω) — Ohm mode
   • Set the dial to "Ω"
   • Touch both probes to the two ends of a resistor
   • The display shows the resistance value
   • IMPORTANT: The component must be DISCONNECTED from the circuit,
     otherwise you'll get a false reading

3. CONTINUITY (🔊) — Beep mode
   • Set the dial to the speaker/diode symbol
   • Touch the probes to two points
   • If there's a connection (very low resistance), it BEEPS
   • This is incredibly useful for checking if wires are connected
     properly, or if a wire is broken

READING RESISTOR COLOR BANDS:
   Resistors have colored bands that encode their value:
   
   Band 1 (1st digit) | Band 2 (2nd digit) | Band 3 (multiplier) | Band 4 (tolerance)
   
   Color codes:
   Black = 0    Brown = 1    Red = 2      Orange = 3
   Yellow = 4   Green = 5    Blue = 6     Violet = 7
   Gray = 8     White = 9
   
   Multiplier: Brown = ×10, Red = ×100, Orange = ×1000, Yellow = ×10000
   Tolerance: Gold = ±5%, Silver = ±10%
   
   Example: Red-Red-Brown-Gold = 2, 2, ×10 = 220Ω ±5%
   Example: Brown-Black-Orange-Gold = 1, 0, ×1000 = 10,000Ω (10kΩ) ±5%

WHY THIS MATTERS FOR ROBOTICS:
   When your robot doesn't work (and it WILL happen), the multimeter is
   how you find the problem. Is the battery dead? Is a wire broken? Is a
   motor getting power? The multimeter answers all of these questions.

🔨 PRACTICAL EXERCISES:

Exercise 1: Measure Battery Voltage
  1. Set multimeter to DC Voltage (V⎓), 20V range
  2. Touch RED probe to battery positive terminal
  3. Touch BLACK probe to battery negative terminal
  4. A fresh 9V battery should read 8.5-9.5V
  5. A "dead" 9V battery typically reads below 7V

Exercise 2: Measure Resistor Values
  1. Set multimeter to Resistance (Ω), 20kΩ range
  2. Take your 220Ω resistor
  3. Touch one probe to each end
  4. Reading should be close to 220 (±5% means 209-231Ω is normal)
  5. Repeat with other resistors — verify they match their color bands

Exercise 3: Measure Voltage in Your Day 1 Circuit
  1. Rebuild your LED + resistor circuit from Day 1
  2. Set multimeter to DC Voltage
  3. Measure voltage across the BATTERY terminals (should be ~9V or ~6V)
  4. Measure voltage across the RESISTOR (should be ~7V with 9V battery)
  5. Measure voltage across the LED (should be ~2V)
  6. NOTICE: Voltage across resistor + voltage across LED ≈ battery voltage
     This is Kirchhoff's Voltage Law — voltages in a loop add up!

Exercise 4: Continuity Testing
  1. Set multimeter to continuity mode (🔊 symbol)
  2. Touch both probes to the same breadboard row — it should BEEP
  3. Touch probes to DIFFERENT rows — no beep (no connection)
  4. Touch probes to the same power rail — it should BEEP
  5. This confirms how breadboard connections work internally

⚠️ TROUBLESHOOTING:
  • Multimeter shows "0L" or "OL": You're on the wrong range. Switch to a
    higher range setting.
  • Resistance reading fluctuates: Your fingers might be touching both
    probes/leads. Finger resistance adds to the reading.
  • Battery reads 0V: Check that you're in DC voltage mode, not AC or Ω.

✅ DAY 2 CHECKPOINT:
  □ Can you accurately measure voltage from a battery?
  □ Can you measure a resistor and get a reading within 5% of expected?
  □ Can you use continuity mode to test connections?
  □ Do you understand Kirchhoff's Voltage Law (voltages in a loop sum to zero)?

If yes to all — you now have the debugging skills for the rest of this journey.

DAY 3: SERIES & PARALLEL CIRCUITS
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

🎯 LEARNING OBJECTIVES:
  • Understand the difference between series and parallel circuits
  • Build both types and observe real differences
  • Learn how voltage and current distribute in each configuration

📖 THEORY:

There are two fundamental ways to connect components:

SERIES CIRCUITS:
   Components are connected end-to-end, forming a single path for current.

   [Battery +] → [LED 1] → [LED 2] → [LED 3] → [Battery −]

   Key properties:
   • Current is the SAME through all components
   • Voltage is DIVIDED among components
   • If one component fails (opens), the ENTIRE circuit stops
   • Total resistance = R1 + R2 + R3 + ...

   Example: Christmas tree lights wired in series — when one bulb burns
   out, the whole string goes dark.

   For LEDs in series with a 9V battery:
   Each LED drops ~2V, so you can run about 3 LEDs in series (3 × 2V = 6V)
   with a 150Ω resistor to drop the remaining 3V.

PARALLEL CIRCUITS:
   Components are connected side-by-side, each having its own path to the
   power source.

   [Battery +] → splits → [LED 1] → joins → [Battery −]
                        → [LED 2] →
                        → [LED 3] →

   Key properties:
   • Voltage is the SAME across all components
   • Current is DIVIDED among branches
   • If one component fails, the others KEEP WORKING
   • Total resistance: 1/Rtotal = 1/R1 + 1/R2 + 1/R3 + ...

   Example: Your home electrical outlets are wired in parallel — turning
   off one lamp doesn't affect the others.

WHY THIS MATTERS FOR ROBOTICS:
   Your robot will use BOTH configurations. Motors are typically wired in
   parallel (so they can be controlled independently). Sensor power lines
   are in parallel. Understanding this lets you design circuits where
   each part works independently — critical for a working robot.

🔨 PRACTICAL: BUILD BOTH CIRCUIT TYPES

Build 1: Three LEDs in Series
  Materials: 3 LEDs, 1 resistor (100-150Ω), battery, breadboard, wires

  1. Place LED 1 anode (long leg) connected to the resistor
  2. Connect the resistor's other end to battery positive (+)
  3. Connect LED 1 cathode (short leg) to LED 2 anode (long leg)
  4. Connect LED 2 cathode to LED 3 anode
  5. Connect LED 3 cathode to battery negative (−)
  6. Observe: All three LEDs light up, but they're DIMMER than a single LED

Build 2: Three LEDs in Parallel
  Materials: 3 LEDs, 3 resistors (220Ω each), battery, breadboard, wires

  1. Connect battery + to the positive power rail
  2. Connect battery − to the negative power rail
  3. For EACH LED: connect a 220Ω resistor from the + rail to the LED anode
  4. Connect each LED cathode to the − rail
  5. Observe: All three LEDs are BRIGHTER than the series circuit
     (each LED gets the full voltage minus its resistor drop)

Experiment:
  • Remove one LED from each circuit
  • Series: remaining LEDs go OUT (circuit is broken)
  • Parallel: remaining LEDs STAY ON (independent paths)

  Use your multimeter to measure:
  • Voltage across each LED in both circuits
  • Series: each LED has ~2V across it
  • Parallel: each LED has ~2V across it, but the current per branch is higher

⚠️ TROUBLESHOOTING:
  • Series LEDs don't light: Ensure all LEDs face the same direction
    (anode → cathode → anode → cathode → ...)
  • Parallel LEDs uneven brightness: Check all resistors are the same value
  • No LEDs light: Check battery voltage with multimeter

✅ DAY 3 CHECKPOINT:
  □ Can you explain the difference between series and parallel?
  □ Did you observe brightness differences?
  □ Can you predict what happens when you remove one component from each type?
  □ Can you identify which type is used in a given circuit diagram?

DAY 4: RESISTORS DEEP DIVE — POWER AND HEAT
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

🎯 LEARNING OBJECTIVES:
  • Understand electrical power and the power formula
  • Learn about resistor power ratings and why they matter
  • Calculate proper resistor values for different LED configurations
  • Understand voltage dividers

📖 THEORY:

POWER (P) — Measured in Watts (W):
   Power is the rate at which energy is converted. In a resistor, electrical
   energy is converted to HEAT.

   P = V × I        (Power = Voltage × Current)
   P = I² × R       (useful when you know current and resistance)
   P = V² / R       (useful when you know voltage and resistance)

   Example: A 220Ω resistor with 20mA flowing through it:
   P = (0.020)² × 220 = 0.088W = 88mW
   Most small resistors are rated for 0.25W (250mW), so this is safe.

   But what if you used a 10Ω resistor with 500mA?
   P = (0.500)² × 10 = 2.5W
   A standard 0.25W resistor would BURN UP. You'd need a power resistor.

RESISTOR POWER RATINGS:
   • Standard small resistors: 1/8W (125mW) or 1/4W (250mW)
   • Power resistors: 1W, 2W, 5W, 10W+ (physically larger)
   • Rule: Always choose a resistor rated for at LEAST 2× the calculated
     power dissipation for safety margin

VOLTAGE DIVIDERS — AN ESSENTIAL CIRCUIT:
   A voltage divider uses two resistors to create an output voltage that is
   a fraction of the input voltage.

               Vin
                |
              [R1]
                |──── Vout
              [R2]
                |
               GND

   Formula: Vout = Vin × (R2 / (R1 + R2))

   Example: Vin = 9V, R1 = 10kΩ, R2 = 10kΩ
   Vout = 9 × (10000 / 20000) = 4.5V

   This is how sensors often output signals, and how you can step down
   voltages for sensitive components.

WHY THIS MATTERS FOR ROBOTICS:
   Motors draw significant current. If your resistor can't handle the power,
   it will overheat and potentially start a fire. Understanding power
   calculations keeps your robot safe. Voltage dividers are used everywhere
   in sensor circuits and battery monitoring.

🔨 PRACTICAL EXERCISES:

Exercise 1: Calculate Resistor for Different LEDs
  Different color LEDs have different forward voltages:
  • Red LED:    ~1.8V forward voltage, 20mA typical current
  • Green LED:  ~2.2V forward voltage, 20mA typical current
  • Blue LED:   ~3.2V forward voltage, 20mA typical current

  With a 9V supply, calculate R for each:
  • Red:   R = (9 - 1.8) / 0.020 = 360Ω → use 390Ω (nearest standard)
  • Green: R = (9 - 2.2) / 0.020 = 340Ω → use 330Ω
  • Blue:  R = (9 - 3.2) / 0.020 = 290Ω → use 300Ω or 330Ω

Exercise 2: Try Different Resistor Values
  With a single red LED and 9V battery, try these resistors:
  • 100Ω — LED is bright (high current: ~72mA — near LED max!)
  • 220Ω — LED is medium brightness (~33mA)
  • 1kΩ  — LED is dim (~7mA)
  • 10kΩ — LED is barely visible (~0.7mA)

  Measure the current with your multimeter (set to mA mode, place in
  SERIES with the circuit) to verify your calculations.

Exercise 3: Build a Voltage Divider
  1. Connect two 10kΩ resistors in series between 9V and ground
  2. Measure the voltage at the junction between them
  3. It should read approximately 4.5V
  4. Try different ratios: 10kΩ + 20kΩ should give 6V at the junction

⚠️ TROUBLESHOOTING:
  • Resistor getting hot: Check power calculations — you may need a higher
    resistance value or a power resistor
  • Voltage divider reading wrong: Make sure nothing else is connected to
    the output point (loads affect the voltage)

✅ DAY 4 CHECKPOINT:
  □ Can you calculate power dissipation in a resistor?
  □ Can you choose the right resistor for any LED color?
  □ Can you build and measure a voltage divider?
  □ Do you understand why power ratings matter?

DAY 5: CAPACITORS — ENERGY STORAGE AND SMOOTHING
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

🎯 LEARNING OBJECTIVES:
  • Understand what capacitors do and how they work
  • Learn the RC time constant
  • Build a circuit demonstrating capacitor charging and discharging
  • Understand why capacitors are essential for stable robot circuits

📖 THEORY:

A capacitor stores electrical energy in an electric field. Think of it as a
tiny rechargeable battery that charges and discharges very quickly.

CAPACITOR BASICS:
  • Measured in Farads (F), but most are microfarads (μF) or nanofarads (nF)
  • Two types you'll use:
    - Electrolytic: Larger values (1μF to 10000μF), POLARIZED (has + and −)
    - Ceramic: Smaller values (0.001μF to 1μF), NOT polarized

  ⚠️ CRITICAL: Electrolytic capacitors MUST be connected with correct
  polarity. The negative leg is marked with a stripe and is shorter.
  Reversing polarity can cause the capacitor to EXPLODE.

HOW CAPACITORS CHARGE:
  When you connect a capacitor to a voltage source through a resistor:
  1. Initially, the capacitor acts like a short circuit (max current flows)
  2. As it charges, current decreases exponentially
  3. Eventually, the capacitor is "full" and no current flows

  The TIME CONSTANT (τ) determines how fast this happens:
  τ = R × C

  After 1τ: capacitor is 63% charged
  After 3τ: capacitor is 95% charged
  After 5τ: capacitor is 99% charged (considered "fully" charged)

  Example: R = 10kΩ, C = 100μF
  τ = 10,000 × 0.000100 = 1 second
  Full charge takes about 5 seconds

DECOUPLING CAPACITORS — THE UNSUNG HEROES:
  In real circuits, power supplies are noisy. Motors starting up cause
  voltage dips. Digital chips switching create high-frequency noise.
  Capacitors placed across the power supply absorb these fluctuations
  and provide stable power.

  Rule of thumb:
  • Place a 100μF electrolytic near the main power input
  • Place a 0.1μF ceramic near each IC (chip) power pin

WHY THIS MATTERS FOR ROBOTICS:
  Without decoupling capacitors, your robot's Arduino will randomly reset
  when motors start. Sensors will give garbage readings. The motor driver
  will behave erratically. Capacitors are the difference between a robot
  that works reliably and one that glitches constantly.

🔨 PRACTICAL EXERCISES:

Exercise 1: Observe Charging and Discharging
  Materials: 1000μF electrolytic capacitor, 10kΩ resistor, LED, 9V battery

  Charging:
  1. Connect the resistor and capacitor in series across the battery
  2. Connect the LED in parallel with the capacitor (with a 220Ω resistor)
  3. When you connect the battery, the LED will start bright and slowly dim
     as the capacitor charges (it absorbs the current)

  Discharging:
  1. Disconnect the battery
  2. The LED should glow for a moment as the capacitor releases its stored
     energy — like a tiny battery!
  3. With a 1000μF capacitor, the LED may glow for 1-2 seconds

Exercise 2: Power Supply Smoothing
  1. Build your basic LED circuit from Day 1
  2. Observe the LED with no capacitor (it should look steady, but
     with sensitive equipment you'd see small fluctuations)
  3. Add a 100μF capacitor across the power rails (+ to +, − to −)
  4. This won't look different with an LED, but when you add motors
     later, this will be critical for stability

⚠️ TROUBLESHOOTING:
  • Capacitor gets hot: WRONG POLARITY — disconnect immediately!
  • Circuit doesn't seem different with capacitor: For LEDs, the effect is
    subtle. The real benefit appears with motors and microcontrollers.
  • LED stays lit after removing power: The capacitor is discharging. This
    is expected behavior!

✅ DAY 5 CHECKPOINT:
  □ Can you identify electrolytic vs ceramic capacitors?
  □ Can you calculate the RC time constant?
  □ Did you observe the capacitor charging and discharging?
  □ Do you understand why decoupling capacitors are needed in robot circuits?

DAY 6: DIODES — ONE-WAY VALVES FOR ELECTRICITY
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

🎯 LEARNING OBJECTIVES:
  • Understand how diodes work and why they're essential
  • Learn about forward voltage drop
  • Build a protection circuit using diodes
  • Understand flyback diodes (critical for motor circuits)

📖 THEORY:

A diode is the simplest semiconductor device. It allows current to flow in
only ONE direction — from anode (+) to cathode (−).

DIODE BASICS:
  • The cathode is marked with a BAND/STRIPE on the component body
  • Forward biased: Current flows (anode is more positive than cathode)
  • Reverse biased: Current is BLOCKED (almost zero current flows)
  • Forward voltage drop: Diodes "use up" a small voltage
    - Silicon diode (1N4007): ~0.7V drop
    - Schottky diode: ~0.3V drop
    - LED: 1.8-3.3V drop (and it glows!)

   Yes — an LED IS a diode! It's a "Light Emitting Diode." That's why it
   only works in one direction.

FLYBACK DIODES — PROTECTING YOUR CIRCUITS FROM MOTORS:
   This is the most important diode application for robotics.

   When a motor is running and you suddenly cut power, the motor's coil
   generates a voltage spike in the REVERSE direction. This spike can be
   50V or more — enough to destroy your Arduino or motor driver.

   A flyback diode is placed ACROSS the motor in REVERSE:
   
   [Motor +]──┬──[Motor]──┬──[Motor −]
              │            │
              └──|◄|───────┘   (Diode: cathode to +, anode to −)

   During normal operation, the diode is reverse-biased (does nothing).
   When the motor stops, the voltage spike forward-biases the diode,
   safely absorbing the spike.

REVERSE POLARITY PROTECTION:
   Place a diode in series with your power input:
   [Battery +] → |►| → [Circuit] → [Battery −]

   If someone connects the battery backwards, the diode blocks current
   and protects your circuit. You lose 0.7V, but gain protection.

WHY THIS MATTERS FOR ROBOTICS:
   Every motor on your robot NEEDS a flyback diode. Without one, the
   first time you stop a motor, the voltage spike can kill your Arduino
   ($5-$25 replacement) or your motor driver ($5-$10 replacement).
   A diode costs $0.05. Always use them.

🔨 PRACTICAL EXERCISES:

Exercise 1: Test Diode Direction
  1. Build a simple LED circuit from Day 1
  2. Add a 1N4007 diode in SERIES, oriented so current can flow
     (band facing away from battery +)
  3. LED lights up — current flows through the diode
  4. REVERSE the diode (flip it around)
  5. LED goes dark — current is blocked
  6. Measure with multimeter: 0V across LED when diode is reversed

Exercise 2: Build Reverse Polarity Protection
  1. Place a diode between the battery + and your circuit
  2. Measure the voltage: it's about 0.7V less than the battery
     (the diode's forward drop)
  3. Reverse the battery connections
  4. Circuit stays OFF — protected!

Exercise 3: Test Flyback Concept (Visual Demo)
  1. If you have a small motor, connect it to your battery through a switch
  2. Connect an LED (with resistor) across the motor terminals
  3. Run the motor, then quickly disconnect power
  4. You may see the LED briefly flash — that's the flyback voltage!
  5. Now add a diode across the motor (cathode to +, anode to −)
  6. The LED flash should be eliminated or greatly reduced

⚠️ TROUBLESHOOTING:
  • Circuit dead after adding diode: Check orientation — the band indicates
    the cathode (negative/blocking end)
  • LED still lights when diode is "reversed": You may have the diode
    orientation confused. The stripe = cathode.

✅ DAY 6 CHECKPOINT:
  □ Can you identify the anode and cathode of a diode?
  □ Do you understand forward voltage drop?
  □ Can you explain why flyback diodes are needed for motors?
  □ Did you build a reverse polarity protection circuit?

DAY 7: WEEK 1 REVIEW + MINI PROJECT
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

🎯 LEARNING OBJECTIVES:
  • Consolidate everything from Week 1
  • Build a complete, stable circuit combining all components
  • Self-assess your understanding before moving to control components

📖 THEORY REVIEW:

Let's review what you've learned:

Day 1: Voltage, Current, Resistance — V = IR
   → You can calculate any value if you know the other two

Day 2: Multimeter — Measure V, R, and continuity
   → You can debug any circuit by measuring what's happening

Day 3: Series & Parallel — Two ways to connect components
   → Series: same current, divided voltage
   → Parallel: same voltage, divided current

Day 4: Power — P = IV = I²R = V²/R
   → You can ensure components won't overheat

Day 5: Capacitors — Store energy, smooth power
   → τ = RC, decoupling prevents glitches

Day 6: Diodes — One-way current flow
   → Flyback protection prevents motor spikes from killing circuits

COMBINING CONCEPTS:
  A well-designed circuit uses ALL of these together:
  • Resistors to limit current
  • Capacitors to smooth power
  • Diodes to protect against spikes
  • Proper series/parallel configurations
  • Correct power calculations to avoid overheating

🔨 PRACTICAL: BUILD THE "STABLE LED INDICATOR" CIRCUIT

This mini project combines everything from Week 1 into one circuit:

Circuit Description:
  A stable, protected LED indicator with power smoothing — the kind of
  sub-circuit you'd find inside any real robot.

Components needed:
  • Breadboard
  • 9V battery with clip
  • 1N4007 diode (reverse polarity protection)
  • 100μF electrolytic capacitor (power smoothing)
  • 0.1μF ceramic capacitor (high-frequency filtering)
  • 220Ω resistor (LED current limiting)
  • LED (any color)
  • Jumper wires

Build Steps:
  1. Connect battery + through a 1N4007 diode to the + power rail
     (band/cathode toward the rail — current flows toward the circuit)
  2. Connect battery − to the − power rail
  3. Place the 100μF electrolytic capacitor across the power rails
     (longer leg to +, shorter leg with stripe to −)
  4. Place the 0.1μF ceramic capacitor also across the power rails
     (these are not polarized, either direction works)
  5. Connect the 220Ω resistor from the + rail to a free row
  6. Connect the LED anode (long leg) to the same row as the resistor
  7. Connect the LED cathode (short leg) to the − rail
  8. Power it up!

What to verify with your multimeter:
  • Battery voltage: ~9V
  • Voltage after the diode: ~8.3V (9V minus 0.7V diode drop)
  • Voltage across the capacitors: ~8.3V (same as supply after diode)
  • Voltage across the resistor: ~6.3V (8.3V - 2V LED drop)
  • Voltage across the LED: ~2V
  • Current through LED: ~29mA (6.3V / 220Ω)

What this circuit demonstrates:
  ✓ Reverse polarity protection (try reversing the battery — nothing
    happens, circuit is protected)
  ✓ Power smoothing (capacitors filter noise)
  ✓ Proper current limiting (resistor protects the LED)
  ✓ Your understanding of voltage drops around the circuit

⚠️ TROUBLESHOOTING:
  • LED doesn't light: Check diode orientation, capacitor polarity, LED
    polarity — use continuity mode to trace the circuit
  • Voltage readings don't match: Check all connections, ensure multimeter
    is in the right mode

DAY 8: TRANSISTORS — ELECTRONIC SWITCHES
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

🎯 LEARNING OBJECTIVES:
  • Understand how a transistor works as a switch
  • Learn the three pins: Base, Collector, Emitter
  • Build a transistor-controlled LED circuit
  • Understand why transistors are essential for motor control

📖 THEORY:

A transistor is a semiconductor device that can act as an electronically
controlled switch. Instead of physically flipping a switch, you apply a
small current to one pin, and the transistor allows a much larger current
to flow through the other two pins.

NPN TRANSISTOR (2N2222 or BC547):
  Three pins (looking at the flat face, left to right):
  • Emitter (E) — connects to ground
  • Base (B) — the "control" pin (middle)
  • Collector (C) — connects to the load (LED, motor, etc.)

  How it works:
  • When NO current flows into the Base → transistor is OFF (open switch)
  • When a small current flows into the Base → transistor turns ON
    (closed switch), allowing large current from Collector to Emitter

  The transistor AMPLIFIES: a tiny base current (1-5mA) controls a much
  larger collector current (up to 500mA+ depending on transistor).

  Current gain (β or hFE): typically 100-300 for small signal transistors
  This means: I_collector = β × I_base

  Example: You want to switch 200mA through a motor
  With β = 200: I_base = 200mA / 200 = 1mA
  Base resistor with 5V control: R = (5V - 0.7V) / 0.001A = 4.3kΩ → use 4.7kΩ

SATURATION vs ACTIVE vs CUTOFF:
  • Cutoff: No base current → transistor fully OFF
  • Active: Some base current → transistor partially ON (used in amplifiers)
  • Saturation: Enough base current → transistor fully ON (used as switch)

  For switching (robotics), we always want SATURATION — fully ON or fully OFF.
  To ensure saturation, use a base current ~10× more than the minimum.

WHY THIS MATTERS FOR ROBOTICS:
  Arduino pins can only supply ~20-40mA. Motors need 200-1000mA+.
  A transistor lets the Arduino's tiny signal control the motor's large
  current. This is the fundamental building block of motor control.

🔨 PRACTICAL: TRANSISTOR-CONTROLLED LED

Materials: 2N2222 transistor, LED, 220Ω resistor, 1kΩ resistor, battery,
           breadboard, wires, push button

Build steps:
  1. Connect the LED + 220Ω resistor in series from battery + to the
     transistor's Collector pin
  2. Connect the transistor's Emitter pin to battery −  (ground)
  3. Connect the 1kΩ resistor from battery + through a push button
     to the transistor's Base pin
  4. Press the button: LED turns ON
  5. Release the button: LED turns OFF

What's happening:
  • Button pressed → current flows through 1kΩ into Base
  • Base current turns transistor ON (saturation)
  • Current flows from Collector to Emitter through the LED
  • Button released → no base current → transistor OFF → LED off

Experiment:
  • Try different base resistor values (10kΩ, 47kΩ)
  • With very high resistance, the LED might be dim (transistor in active
    region, not fully saturated)
  • With appropriate resistance (1kΩ-4.7kΩ), the LED is fully bright

⚠️ TROUBLESHOOTING:
  • LED always on: Check transistor orientation — pins may be in wrong order
  • LED never turns on: Verify pin identification. Look up your transistor's
    datasheet for the correct pinout (E-B-C order varies by model!)
  • Transistor gets hot: Base resistor may be too low, or you're exceeding
    the transistor's current rating

DAY 9: MOTORS + TRANSISTORS — YOUR FIRST ROBOTICS MOMENT
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

🎯 LEARNING OBJECTIVES:
  • Control a DC motor using a transistor
  • Understand back-EMF and why flyback diodes are critical
  • Build a protected motor control circuit
  • Experience the thrill of electronically controlling motion

📖 THEORY:

DC MOTORS — HOW THEY WORK:
  A DC motor converts electrical energy into rotational motion using
  electromagnetic principles. When current flows through a coil inside
  a magnetic field, the coil experiences a force that makes it spin.

  Key properties:
  • Two terminals (no polarity marking usually) — reversing polarity
    reverses rotation direction
  • Stall current: the maximum current drawn when the motor can't spin
    (shaft is held). This can be 5-10× the running current!
  • Running current: current drawn during normal operation (typically
    100-500mA for small hobby motors)
  • Voltage rating: typically 3-6V for small hobby motors

  ⚠️ NEVER connect a motor directly to an Arduino pin!
  Arduino pins: max ~40mA at 5V
  Motor needs: 200-1000mA+ at 3-6V
  Result: Damaged Arduino, sad student

BACK-EMF — THE HIDDEN DANGER:
  When you cut power to a spinning motor, the motor briefly becomes a
  GENERATOR. The spinning momentum creates a reverse voltage spike
  (back-EMF) that can reach 50-100V for a fraction of a second.

  This spike travels back through your circuit and can destroy:
  • Your transistor
  • Your Arduino
  • Your motor driver

  Solution: ALWAYS place a flyback diode across the motor (from Day 6):
  Cathode to motor +, anode to motor −. The diode absorbs the spike.

🔨 PRACTICAL: MOTOR CONTROL WITH TRANSISTOR + PROTECTION

Materials: DC motor (3-6V), 2N2222 transistor (or TIP120 for larger motors),
           1N4007 diode, 1kΩ resistor, push button, battery (4xAA = 6V),
           100μF capacitor, breadboard, wires

Build steps:
  1. Connect the motor between battery + and the Collector of the transistor
  2. Connect the transistor Emitter to battery − (ground)
  3. Place a 1N4007 diode ACROSS the motor:
     - Diode CATHODE (band) to the motor terminal connected to battery +
     - Diode ANODE to the motor terminal connected to Collector
  4. Connect 1kΩ resistor from battery + through push button to Base
  5. Place 100μF capacitor across power rails for stability
  6. Press the button — THE MOTOR SPINS! 🎉

This is your FIRST real robotics moment. You just electronically controlled
a motor using a semiconductor. This is exactly how robots work — electronic
signals controlling mechanical motion.

Experiment:
  • Hold the motor shaft gently and feel the torque
  • Listen for the pitch change when you load the motor
  • Remove the flyback diode and notice if the transistor gets warm when
    stopping the motor (put it back immediately!)
  • Try powering from different voltages (3V vs 6V) — observe speed change

⚠️ TROUBLESHOOTING:
  • Motor doesn't spin: Check transistor orientation, check motor works
    by connecting directly to battery briefly
  • Motor spins but is weak: Battery might be low, or transistor might be
    in active mode (not saturated) — try a smaller base resistor
  • Transistor gets very hot: Motor may draw too much current for a 2N2222
    (max ~800mA). Use a TIP120 Darlington transistor for motors over 500mA

✅ DAY 9 CHECKPOINT:

DAY 10: PWM — CONTROLLING MOTOR SPEED
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

🎯 LEARNING OBJECTIVES:
  • Understand Pulse Width Modulation (PWM)
  • Learn duty cycle and its effect on average voltage
  • Control motor speed using PWM concept
  • Prepare for Arduino PWM control

📖 THEORY:

PWM — PULSE WIDTH MODULATION:
  Instead of reducing voltage to slow a motor (which reduces torque), PWM
  rapidly switches the motor ON and OFF. The motor's inertia smooths this
  out, and the motor "feels" an average voltage.

  How it works:
  • The signal switches between fully ON (high) and fully OFF (low)
  • The fraction of time spent ON is called the DUTY CYCLE
  • The motor responds to the AVERAGE voltage

  Duty Cycle examples:
  • 100% duty cycle = always ON = full speed
  • 50% duty cycle = ON half the time = ~half speed
  • 25% duty cycle = ON quarter of time = ~quarter speed
  • 0% duty cycle = always OFF = stopped

  Formula: V_average = V_supply × (duty_cycle / 100)

  Example: 6V supply, 50% duty cycle
  V_average = 6V × 0.50 = 3V average → motor runs at roughly half speed

  PWM FREQUENCY:
  The switching happens very fast — typically 500Hz to 25kHz.
  • Too slow (<100Hz): Motor physically stutters, audible clicking
  • Sweet spot (1kHz-20kHz): Smooth motor operation
  • Too fast (>25kHz): Some transistors can't switch fast enough

WHY PWM IS BETTER THAN VOLTAGE REDUCTION:
  • A motor at 50% PWM with 6V supply has FULL torque during its ON pulses
  • A motor at steady 3V has reduced torque at all times
  • PWM gives better low-speed performance and efficiency

WHY THIS MATTERS FOR ROBOTICS:
  Every real robot uses PWM for motor control. When your robot needs to
  slow down for a turn or navigate carefully, it uses PWM. The Arduino
  has built-in PWM outputs, making this easy to implement.

🔨 PRACTICAL: MANUAL PWM DEMONSTRATION

For now, without an Arduino, you can understand PWM conceptually:

Exercise 1: Manual PWM (Hands-On Feel)
  Using your transistor motor circuit from Day 9:
  1. Rapidly press and release the button — try for ~2 times per second
  2. The motor will stutter and spin slowly
  3. Press and hold for longer intervals — motor speeds up
  4. This is "manual PWM" — you're varying the duty cycle by hand

Exercise 2: Understand the Math
  Calculate average voltages for a 6V supply:
  • 10% duty cycle: 6V × 0.10 = 0.6V (motor barely turns)
  • 30% duty cycle: 6V × 0.30 = 1.8V (slow rotation)
  • 50% duty cycle: 6V × 0.50 = 3.0V (medium speed)
  • 75% duty cycle: 6V × 0.75 = 4.5V (fast)
  • 100% duty cycle: 6V × 1.00 = 6.0V (full speed)

  When we get to Arduino, you'll use analogWrite(pin, value) where
  value is 0-255:
  • analogWrite(pin, 0) = 0% duty cycle
  • analogWrite(pin, 127) = ~50% duty cycle
  • analogWrite(pin, 255) = 100% duty cycle

DAY 11: POTENTIOMETER — VARIABLE RESISTANCE
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

🎯 LEARNING OBJECTIVES:
  • Understand how potentiometers work
  • Use a potentiometer to control LED brightness
  • Learn the relationship between potentiometers and voltage dividers
  • Prepare for analog sensor reading concepts

📖 THEORY:

A potentiometer (pot) is a three-terminal variable resistor. Turning the
knob changes the resistance between the terminals.

HOW IT WORKS:
  A potentiometer has three pins:
  • Pin 1: One end of the resistive element
  • Pin 2: The wiper (movable contact) — this is the OUTPUT
  • Pin 3: Other end of the resistive element

  Total resistance (Pin 1 to Pin 3) is fixed (e.g., 10kΩ).
  Turning the knob moves the wiper, changing:
  • Resistance from Pin 1 to Pin 2
  • Resistance from Pin 2 to Pin 3
  • These two always add up to the total resistance

  A potentiometer IS a variable voltage divider!

  If you connect Pin 1 to 5V and Pin 3 to GND:
  • Turning fully to Pin 1: Pin 2 outputs ~5V
  • Turning to middle: Pin 2 outputs ~2.5V
  • Turning fully to Pin 3: Pin 2 outputs ~0V

WHY THIS MATTERS FOR ROBOTICS:
  Potentiometers are used for:
  • Manual speed control of motors
  • Setting threshold values
  • Joystick inputs (joysticks are just two potentiometers!)
  • Understanding how analog sensors output variable voltages

🔨 PRACTICAL: POTENTIOMETER-CONTROLLED LED

Materials: 10kΩ potentiometer, LED, 220Ω resistor, battery, breadboard

Build steps:
  1. Connect battery + to Pot Pin 1
  2. Connect battery − to Pot Pin 3
  3. Connect Pot Pin 2 (wiper/middle) through a 220Ω resistor to the LED anode
  4. Connect LED cathode to battery −
  5. Turn the knob — LED brightness changes smoothly!

Experiment:
  • Use your multimeter to measure voltage at Pin 2 as you turn the knob
  • You should see voltage change smoothly from ~0V to ~battery voltage
  • Note: the 220Ω resistor is still important for current limiting

Advanced experiment:
  • Replace the LED circuit with your motor circuit from Day 9
  • Connect Pot Pin 2 to the transistor Base through a 1kΩ resistor
  • Turning the pot should change motor speed (though not smoothly —
    the transistor acts more like a switch than a variable controller
    in this simple configuration)

⚠️ TROUBLESHOOTING:
  • No brightness change: Check pot wiring — middle pin must be the output
  • LED stays off: Pot may be turned to minimum, or wiring is incorrect
  • Pot gets warm: Don't use low-value pots (under 1kΩ) across the full
    battery voltage — too much current flows through the resistive element

DAY 12: ANALOG VS DIGITAL SIGNALS
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

🎯 LEARNING OBJECTIVES:
  • Understand the fundamental difference between analog and digital
  • Learn how microcontrollers convert between analog and digital (ADC/DAC)
  • Recognize which sensors produce which type of signal
  • Prepare for Arduino analog and digital I/O

📖 THEORY:

DIGITAL SIGNALS:
  A digital signal has only two states: HIGH (ON) or LOW (OFF).
  • In Arduino terms: HIGH = 5V, LOW = 0V
  • Like a light switch — it's either on or off, nothing in between
  • Examples: push buttons, switches, IR obstacle sensors (detect/no detect)

  Digital is perfect for YES/NO questions:
  • Is the button pressed? YES or NO
  • Is something blocking the sensor? YES or NO
  • Should the LED be on? YES or NO

ANALOG SIGNALS:
  An analog signal can be ANY value within a range — it's continuous.
  • Like a dimmer switch — it can be anywhere from off to full brightness
  • Examples: temperature sensors, potentiometers, light sensors
  • In the real world, almost everything is analog

  Analog answers "how much" questions:
  • How far away is the obstacle? 5cm, 15cm, 100cm...
  • How hot is it? 20°C, 25°C, 37.5°C...
  • How bright is the light? Very dim, medium, blinding...

ANALOG-TO-DIGITAL CONVERSION (ADC):
  Microcontrollers are digital devices — they can only understand 0s and 1s.
  To read an analog signal, they use an ADC (Analog-to-Digital Converter).

  Arduino Uno's ADC:
  • 10-bit resolution: converts 0-5V to a number from 0 to 1023
  • 0V input → reads 0
  • 2.5V input → reads ~512
  • 5V input → reads 1023

  Resolution: 5V / 1024 = ~4.9mV per step
  This means the Arduino can distinguish voltage changes of ~5mV.

  Code: int value = analogRead(A0);  // Returns 0-1023

DIGITAL-TO-ANALOG (PWM as DAC):
  Arduino doesn't have a true DAC, but PWM simulates analog output.
  analogWrite(pin, 127) creates a 50% PWM signal that "averages" to ~2.5V.

WHY THIS MATTERS FOR ROBOTICS:
  Your robot needs to read sensors (analog) AND control outputs (digital/PWM).
  Understanding the difference tells you which Arduino pins to use and how
  to interpret the data you receive.

🔨 PRACTICAL: CONCEPTUAL EXERCISES

Exercise 1: Classify Signals
  For each item, decide if it's analog or digital:
  • Room temperature → ANALOG (continuous range)
  • Light switch → DIGITAL (on/off)
  • Car speedometer → ANALOG (0 to max speed)
  • Door sensor → DIGITAL (open/closed)
  • Volume knob → ANALOG (0 to max)
  • Keyboard key → DIGITAL (pressed/not pressed)

Exercise 2: Calculate ADC Values
  For Arduino's 10-bit ADC (0-1023 for 0-5V):
  • What value for 1V?   → 1023 × (1/5) = 205
  • What value for 3.3V? → 1023 × (3.3/5) = 675
  • What voltage for ADC value 800? → 5 × (800/1023) = 3.91V

Exercise 3: Hands-On Preview
  Using your potentiometer from Day 11:
  • Connect Pot Pin 1 to battery +, Pin 3 to battery −
  • Measure Pin 2 voltage with multimeter as you turn the knob
  • This ANALOG voltage is exactly what the Arduino will read!
  • Imagine the multimeter reading being converted to a 0-1023 number

DAY 13: INTRODUCTION TO MICROCONTROLLERS — MEET THE ARDUINO
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

🎯 LEARNING OBJECTIVES:
  • Understand what a microcontroller is and how it differs from a computer
  • Set up the Arduino IDE software
  • Upload your first program (Blink)
  • Understand the basic structure of an Arduino program

📖 THEORY:

WHAT IS A MICROCONTROLLER?
  A microcontroller is a tiny computer on a single chip. It has:
  • A processor (CPU) — executes your code
  • Memory (RAM + Flash) — stores your program and data
  • Input/Output pins — connects to the physical world
  • Built-in peripherals — ADC, timers, serial communication

  Unlike your PC, a microcontroller:
  • Has NO operating system — your code runs directly on the hardware
  • Runs ONE program at a time — whatever you uploaded
  • Has very limited memory — Arduino Uno: 32KB Flash, 2KB RAM
  • Starts running your code immediately when powered on
  • Is designed to interact with the physical world

THE ARDUINO UNO R3:
  The Arduino Uno is the most popular beginner microcontroller board.

  Key specifications:
  • Microcontroller: ATmega328P
  • Clock speed: 16MHz (16 million operations per second)
  • Digital I/O pins: 14 (6 can do PWM, marked with ~)
  • Analog input pins: 6 (A0-A5)
  • Flash memory: 32KB (for your program)
  • RAM: 2KB (for variables while running)
  • Operating voltage: 5V
  • Input voltage: 7-12V (via barrel jack) or 5V (via USB)
  • Max current per pin: 20mA (absolute max 40mA)

  Important pins:
  • Pin 13: Has a built-in LED on the board
  • 5V pin: Outputs regulated 5V (for sensors)
  • 3.3V pin: Outputs regulated 3.3V (for some sensors)
  • GND: Ground connections (there are several)
  • Vin: Raw input voltage (when using external power)

ARDUINO PROGRAM STRUCTURE:
  Every Arduino program (called a "sketch") has two required functions:

  void setup() {
    // Runs ONCE when Arduino powers on or resets
    // Use for: setting pin modes, initializing serial, one-time setup
  }

  void loop() {
    // Runs FOREVER, over and over, after setup() completes
    // Use for: reading sensors, controlling outputs, main logic
  }

  Key functions you'll use:
  • pinMode(pin, MODE) — Set a pin as INPUT or OUTPUT
  • digitalWrite(pin, HIGH/LOW) — Set a digital pin ON or OFF
  • digitalRead(pin) — Read a digital pin (returns HIGH or LOW)
  • analogRead(pin) — Read an analog pin (returns 0-1023)
  • analogWrite(pin, value) — PWM output (0-255)
  • delay(ms) — Wait for milliseconds
  • Serial.begin(9600) — Start serial communication
  • Serial.println(data) — Print data to serial monitor

🔨 PRACTICAL: SETUP & BLINK

Step 1: Install Arduino IDE
  1. Go to https://www.arduino.cc/en/software
  2. Download Arduino IDE 2.x for your operating system
  3. Install and launch it

Step 2: Connect Arduino
  1. Plug Arduino Uno into your computer with the USB cable
  2. In Arduino IDE: Tools → Board → Arduino Uno
  3. In Arduino IDE: Tools → Port → select the COM port that appeared
     (Windows: COM3, COM4, etc.)

Step 3: Upload Blink
  1. File → Examples → 01.Basics → Blink
  2. This opens the Blink sketch:

     void setup() {
       pinMode(LED_BUILTIN, OUTPUT);    // Pin 13 as output
     }

     void loop() {
       digitalWrite(LED_BUILTIN, HIGH); // LED ON
       delay(1000);                     // Wait 1 second
       digitalWrite(LED_BUILTIN, LOW);  // LED OFF
       delay(1000);                     // Wait 1 second
     }

  3. Click the Upload button (→ arrow)
  4. Wait for "Done uploading"
  5. The built-in LED on pin 13 should blink once per second!

Step 4: Modify and Experiment
  • Change delay(1000) to delay(200) — LED blinks faster
  • Change delay(1000) to delay(2000) — LED blinks slower
  • Try different ON vs OFF times: delay(100) then delay(900) for a
    quick flash effect

Step 5: Blink an External LED
  1. Connect a 220Ω resistor from Arduino pin 8 to LED anode
  2. Connect LED cathode to Arduino GND
  3. Modify the code:
     Change LED_BUILTIN to 8 everywhere
  4. Upload — your external LED now blinks!

⚠️ TROUBLESHOOTING:
  • "Board not found": Try a different USB cable (some are charge-only)
  • "Port not available": Install Arduino drivers, or try a different USB port
  • Upload fails: Make sure correct Board and Port are selected
  • LED doesn't blink: Check wiring polarity (long leg = anode = positive)

DAY 14: DIGITAL INPUTS + OUTPUTS — BUTTON-CONTROLLED LED
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

🎯 LEARNING OBJECTIVES:
  • Read digital inputs (buttons) with Arduino
  • Combine inputs with outputs for interactive control
  • Understand pull-up and pull-down resistors
  • Write your first interactive Arduino program

📖 THEORY:

READING A BUTTON:
  A push button has two states: pressed (connected) or released (disconnected).
  In Arduino terms: pressed = LOW or HIGH depending on wiring.

PULL-UP AND PULL-DOWN RESISTORS:
  When a button is NOT pressed, the input pin is "floating" — it's not
  connected to anything, so it picks up random electrical noise and gives
  unpredictable readings.

  Solution: Use a pull-up or pull-down resistor.

  PULL-DOWN (pin defaults to LOW when button is open):
    [5V] → [Button] → [Arduino Pin]
                          |
                       [10kΩ]
                          |
                        [GND]

    Button released: pin reads LOW (pulled to GND through 10kΩ)
    Button pressed: pin reads HIGH (connected directly to 5V)

  PULL-UP (pin defaults to HIGH when button is open):
    [GND] → [Button] → [Arduino Pin]
                           |
                        [10kΩ]
                           |
                         [5V]

    Button released: pin reads HIGH (pulled to 5V through 10kΩ)
    Button pressed: pin reads LOW (connected directly to GND)

  INTERNAL PULL-UP:
    Arduino has built-in pull-up resistors! You can activate them:
    pinMode(pin, INPUT_PULLUP);
    Then you only need the button between the pin and GND (no external resistor).

DEBOUNCING:
  Mechanical buttons "bounce" — when pressed, the contact rapidly opens
  and closes for a few milliseconds. This can cause multiple triggers.
  Simple fix: add a small delay(50) after detecting a press.

🔨 PRACTICAL: BUTTON → LED CONTROL

Materials: Push button, LED, 220Ω resistor, 10kΩ resistor, Arduino, wires

Build 1: Basic Button LED (with external pull-down)
  Wiring:
  1. LED + 220Ω resistor from pin 8 to GND
  2. Button: one side to 5V, other side to pin 2
  3. 10kΩ resistor from pin 2 to GND (pull-down)

  Code:
    const int buttonPin = 2;
    const int ledPin = 8;

    void setup() {
      pinMode(buttonPin, INPUT);
      pinMode(ledPin, OUTPUT);
    }

    void loop() {
      int buttonState = digitalRead(buttonPin);

      if (buttonState == HIGH) {
        digitalWrite(ledPin, HIGH);  // Button pressed → LED on
      } else {
        digitalWrite(ledPin, LOW);   // Button released → LED off
      }
    }

Build 2: Toggle LED (press to toggle on/off)
  Code:
    const int buttonPin = 2;
    const int ledPin = 8;
    bool ledState = false;
    bool lastButtonState = LOW;

    void setup() {
      pinMode(buttonPin, INPUT);
      pinMode(ledPin, OUTPUT);
    }

    void loop() {
      bool currentButtonState = digitalRead(buttonPin);

      if (currentButtonState == HIGH && lastButtonState == LOW) {
        ledState = !ledState;                // Toggle LED state
        digitalWrite(ledPin, ledState ? HIGH : LOW);
        delay(50);                           // Debounce
      }

      lastButtonState = currentButtonState;
    }

Build 3: Using Internal Pull-Up (simpler wiring)
  Wiring:
  1. LED + 220Ω from pin 8 to GND
  2. Button from pin 2 to GND (NO external resistor needed!)

  Code:
    const int buttonPin = 2;
    const int ledPin = 8;

    void setup() {
      pinMode(buttonPin, INPUT_PULLUP); // Internal pull-up!
      pinMode(ledPin, OUTPUT);
    }

    void loop() {
      if (digitalRead(buttonPin) == LOW) {  // LOW = pressed with pull-up
        digitalWrite(ledPin, HIGH);
      } else {
        digitalWrite(ledPin, LOW);
      }
    }

  Note: With INPUT_PULLUP, the logic is INVERTED — LOW means pressed.

⚠️ TROUBLESHOOTING:
  • LED flickers randomly: You forgot the pull-down/pull-up resistor
  • Button seems to trigger multiple times: Add debounce delay
  • Nothing happens: Check button wiring, try the internal pull-up version

DAY 15: READING SENSORS WITH ARDUINO — analogRead()
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

🎯 LEARNING OBJECTIVES:
  • Use analogRead() to read a potentiometer
  • Display sensor values on the Serial Monitor
  • Understand the mapping from voltage to 0-1023 values
  • Use the map() function to scale values

📖 THEORY:

ANALOG INPUT ON ARDUINO:
  Arduino Uno has 6 analog input pins: A0 through A5.
  Each converts a 0-5V signal into a number from 0 to 1023.

  int sensorValue = analogRead(A0);
  // sensorValue is now 0-1023

SERIAL MONITOR — YOUR DEBUGGING WINDOW:
  The Serial Monitor lets your Arduino send data back to your computer.
  This is invaluable for debugging and understanding sensor readings.

  Setup:  Serial.begin(9600);       // In setup(), start at 9600 baud
  Print:  Serial.println(value);    // Print value + newline
  Open:   Tools → Serial Monitor (or Ctrl+Shift+M)

THE map() FUNCTION:
  Converts a value from one range to another:
  int output = map(input, fromLow, fromHigh, toLow, toHigh);

  Example: Convert 0-1023 to 0-255 (for PWM):
  int pwmValue = map(sensorValue, 0, 1023, 0, 255);

🔨 PRACTICAL: POTENTIOMETER READING

Wiring:
  1. Pot Pin 1 → Arduino 5V
  2. Pot Pin 2 (wiper) → Arduino A0
  3. Pot Pin 3 → Arduino GND

Code:
  void setup() {
    Serial.begin(9600);
  }

  void loop() {
    int sensorValue = analogRead(A0);
    float voltage = sensorValue * (5.0 / 1023.0);

    Serial.print("Raw: ");
    Serial.print(sensorValue);
    Serial.print("  Voltage: ");
    Serial.print(voltage);
    Serial.println("V");

    delay(100);
  }

Open Serial Monitor and turn the potentiometer:
  • Fully counterclockwise: Raw ≈ 0, Voltage ≈ 0.00V
  • Middle: Raw ≈ 512, Voltage ≈ 2.50V
  • Fully clockwise: Raw ≈ 1023, Voltage ≈ 5.00V

    Serial.print(sensorValue);

DAY 16: ULTRASONIC SENSOR — MEASURING DISTANCE
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

🎯 LEARNING OBJECTIVES:
  • Understand how ultrasonic distance sensing works
  • Wire and program the HC-SR04 sensor
  • Calculate distance from time-of-flight
  • Build a distance measurement system

📖 THEORY:

HOW ULTRASONIC SENSING WORKS:
  The HC-SR04 sensor works like a bat's echolocation:
  1. It sends out a burst of ultrasonic sound (40kHz — inaudible to humans)
  2. The sound bounces off an object and returns
  3. The sensor measures the TIME between sending and receiving
  4. We calculate distance using the speed of sound

  Speed of sound in air: ~343 m/s (or 0.0343 cm/μs)

  Distance = (speed × time) / 2
  (Divided by 2 because the sound travels TO the object AND BACK)

  In practical terms:
  Distance (cm) = duration (μs) × 0.0343 / 2
  Distance (cm) = duration (μs) / 58

HC-SR04 SPECIFICATIONS:
  • 4 pins: VCC, Trig, Echo, GND
  • Range: 2cm to 400cm
  • Accuracy: ~3mm
  • Operating voltage: 5V
  • Trigger: Send a 10μs HIGH pulse
  • Echo: Returns a pulse whose width = round-trip time

TIMING WITH pulseIn():
  Arduino's pulseIn(pin, HIGH) function measures how long a pin stays HIGH.
  It returns the duration in microseconds. This is exactly what we need
  to measure the Echo pulse width.

🔨 PRACTICAL: DISTANCE MEASUREMENT SYSTEM

Wiring:
  • VCC → Arduino 5V
  • GND → Arduino GND
  • Trig → Arduino pin 9
  • Echo → Arduino pin 10

Code:
  const int trigPin = 9;
  const int echoPin = 10;

  void setup() {
    pinMode(trigPin, OUTPUT);
    pinMode(echoPin, INPUT);
    Serial.begin(9600);
    Serial.println("Ultrasonic Distance Sensor");
    Serial.println("==========================");
  }

  void loop() {
    // Send trigger pulse
    digitalWrite(trigPin, LOW);
    delayMicroseconds(2);
    digitalWrite(trigPin, HIGH);
    delayMicroseconds(10);
    digitalWrite(trigPin, LOW);

    // Read echo pulse duration
    long duration = pulseIn(echoPin, HIGH);

    // Calculate distance
    float distance = duration / 58.0;

    // Display result
    if (distance > 0 && distance < 400) {
      Serial.print("Distance: ");
      Serial.print(distance);
      Serial.println(" cm");
    } else {
      Serial.println("Out of range");
    }

    delay(200);
  }

Experiment:
  • Place objects at known distances and verify accuracy
  • Try soft vs hard surfaces (soft surfaces absorb sound)
  • Test the minimum range (~2cm) and maximum range (~400cm)
  • Try angling the sensor — notice how accuracy drops at steep angles

⚠️ TROUBLESHOOTING:
  • Always reads 0: Check wiring — Trig and Echo may be swapped
  • Readings are erratic: Add a 100μF capacitor across VCC and GND
  • Maximum range seems short: Sensor may need a clear line of sight

DAY 17: MOTOR DRIVER MODULE — THE L298N
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

🎯 LEARNING OBJECTIVES:
  • Understand why direct motor control with Arduino is dangerous
  • Learn how an H-Bridge motor driver works
  • Wire and test the L298N motor driver
  • Control motor direction and speed

📖 THEORY:

WHY YOU NEED A MOTOR DRIVER:
  Arduino pins: max 40mA at 5V = 0.2W
  Typical DC motor: 200-500mA at 6V = 1.2-3W

  A motor driver is a power amplifier for motors. It takes small control
  signals from Arduino and switches high-current power to the motors.

THE H-BRIDGE:
  An H-Bridge is a circuit that allows current to flow through a motor
  in EITHER direction, enabling forward AND reverse rotation.

  It uses 4 switches (transistors) arranged in an "H" shape:
      [+V]         [+V]
       |             |
      [S1]         [S2]
       |──[MOTOR]──|
      [S3]         [S4]
       |             |
      [GND]        [GND]

  • S1+S4 ON, S2+S3 OFF → Current flows left to right → Motor FORWARD
  • S2+S3 ON, S1+S4 OFF → Current flows right to left → Motor REVERSE
  • All OFF → Motor stops (coasts)
  • S1+S3 ON or S2+S4 ON → SHORT CIRCUIT — NEVER do this!

L298N MODULE PINS:
  Power:
  • +12V (or +V): Motor power supply (6-12V)
  • GND: Common ground (MUST connect to Arduino GND too!)
  • +5V: Can OUTPUT 5V to power Arduino (if jumper is in place and V > 7V)

  Motor outputs:
  • OUT1, OUT2: Motor A terminals
  • OUT3, OUT4: Motor B terminals

  Control inputs:
  • IN1, IN2: Control Motor A direction
  • IN3, IN4: Control Motor B direction
  • ENA: Enable/Speed control for Motor A (PWM)
  • ENB: Enable/Speed control for Motor B (PWM)

  Direction truth table (Motor A):
  • IN1=HIGH, IN2=LOW  → Forward
  • IN1=LOW,  IN2=HIGH → Reverse
  • IN1=LOW,  IN2=LOW  → Stop
  • IN1=HIGH, IN2=HIGH → Brake (motor actively resists motion)

🔨 PRACTICAL: MOTOR DRIVER TESTING

Wiring:
  L298N          Arduino
  IN1         →  pin 5
  IN2         →  pin 4
  ENA         →  pin 6 (PWM pin!)
  GND         →  GND
  +12V        →  Battery + (6V from 4xAA)
  Motor wires →  OUT1, OUT2

Code:
  const int in1 = 5;
  const int in2 = 4;
  const int enA = 6;

  void setup() {
    pinMode(in1, OUTPUT);
    pinMode(in2, OUTPUT);
    pinMode(enA, OUTPUT);
    Serial.begin(9600);
  }

  void loop() {
    // Forward at full speed
    Serial.println("Forward - Full Speed");
    digitalWrite(in1, HIGH);
    digitalWrite(in2, LOW);
    analogWrite(enA, 255);
    delay(2000);

    // Forward at half speed
    Serial.println("Forward - Half Speed");
    analogWrite(enA, 127);
    delay(2000);

    // Stop
    Serial.println("Stop");
    digitalWrite(in1, LOW);
    digitalWrite(in2, LOW);
    delay(1000);

    // Reverse at full speed
    Serial.println("Reverse - Full Speed");
    digitalWrite(in1, LOW);
    digitalWrite(in2, HIGH);
    analogWrite(enA, 255);
    delay(2000);

    // Stop
    Serial.println("Stop");
    digitalWrite(in1, LOW);
    digitalWrite(in2, LOW);
    delay(2000);
  }

⚠️ TROUBLESHOOTING:
  • Motor doesn't spin: Check GND connection between L298N and Arduino
  • Motor only goes one direction: Check IN1/IN2 wiring
  • Speed control doesn't work: Make sure ENA is connected to a PWM pin (~)

SENSOR-ACTUATOR LOOP:

  3. ACT — Control something in the physical world (motor)

🔨 PRACTICAL: DISTANCE-REACTIVE MOTOR

Wiring: Combine Day 16 (ultrasonic) and Day 17 (motor driver) circuits.

Code:
  const int trigPin = 9;
  const int echoPin = 10;
  const int in1 = 5;
  const int in2 = 4;
  const int enA = 6;

  void setup() {
    pinMode(trigPin, OUTPUT);
    pinMode(echoPin, INPUT);
    pinMode(in1, OUTPUT);
    pinMode(in2, OUTPUT);
    pinMode(enA, OUTPUT);
    Serial.begin(9600);
  }

  float getDistance() {
    digitalWrite(trigPin, LOW);
    delayMicroseconds(2);
    digitalWrite(trigPin, HIGH);
    delayMicroseconds(10);
    digitalWrite(trigPin, LOW);
    long duration = pulseIn(echoPin, HIGH);
    return duration / 58.0;
  }

    float distance = getDistance();
    Serial.print("Distance: ");
    Serial.print(distance);
    Serial.print(" cm → ");

    if (distance > 30) {
      // Far away — full speed forward
      Serial.println("FULL SPEED");
      digitalWrite(in1, HIGH);
      digitalWrite(in2, LOW);
      analogWrite(enA, 255);
    } else if (distance > 15) {
      // Getting close — slow down
      int speed = map(distance, 15, 30, 100, 255);
      Serial.print("SLOWING: speed=");
      Serial.println(speed);
      digitalWrite(in1, HIGH);
      digitalWrite(in2, LOW);
      analogWrite(enA, speed);
    } else {
      // Too close — stop!
      Serial.println("STOP!");
      digitalWrite(in1, LOW);
      digitalWrite(in2, LOW);
      analogWrite(enA, 0);
    }
    delay(100);
  }

DAY 19: NOISE & STABILITY — MAKING CIRCUITS RELIABLE
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

🎯 LEARNING OBJECTIVES:
  • Understand electrical noise and its sources
  • Learn how to filter and reduce noise
  • Apply capacitors and good wiring practices
  • Make your sensor readings more reliable

📖 THEORY:

WHAT IS ELECTRICAL NOISE?
  Noise is unwanted electrical signals that interfere with your circuit.
  Sources include:
  • Motors (generate electromagnetic interference when spinning)
  • Power supply fluctuations (battery voltage drops under load)
  • Long wires (act as antennas picking up interference)
  • Switching components (transistors, motor drivers)

NOISE SYMPTOMS IN ROBOTICS:
  • Arduino randomly resets when motors start
  • Sensor readings jump around erratically
  • Serial communication becomes garbled
  • Motors behave unpredictably

SOLUTIONS:

  1. DECOUPLING CAPACITORS (Day 5 revisited):
     • 100μF electrolytic across motor power supply
     • 0.1μF ceramic across Arduino VCC and GND
     • 0.1μF ceramic across sensor VCC and GND

  2. SEPARATE POWER SUPPLIES:
     • Motors should ideally have their own power source
     • Arduino + sensors on USB or separate regulated supply
     • Common ground between all supplies is ESSENTIAL

  3. SOFTWARE FILTERING:
     Average multiple sensor readings to smooth out noise:

     float getAverageDistance(int samples) {
       float total = 0;
       for (int i = 0; i < samples; i++) {
         total += getDistance();
         delay(10);
       }
       return total / samples;
     }

  4. GOOD WIRING PRACTICES:
     • Keep motor wires away from sensor wires
     • Use short wires whenever possible
     • Twist power and ground wires together
     • Solder connections when possible (breadboards add resistance)

🔨 PRACTICAL: ADD STABILITY TO YOUR DAY 18 CIRCUIT

  1. Add a 100μF capacitor across the motor power supply
  2. Add a 0.1μF ceramic capacitor across Arduino 5V and GND
  3. Add a 0.1μF ceramic capacitor across the ultrasonic sensor VCC and GND
  4. Implement software averaging in your distance reading
  5. Compare readings before and after — they should be smoother

DAY 20: DEBUGGING SKILLS — FINDING AND FIXING PROBLEMS

📖 THEORY:

THE DEBUGGING PROCESS:
  1. OBSERVE: What exactly is happening vs what should happen?
  2. HYPOTHESIZE: What could cause this difference?
  3. TEST: Check one thing at a time
  4. FIX: Apply the solution
  5. VERIFY: Confirm the fix works AND nothing else broke

COMMON HARDWARE PROBLEMS:
  • Loose breadboard connections: Wiggle wires while circuit runs
  • Wrong pin connections: Double-check every wire against your diagram
  • Dead battery: Measure voltage with multimeter
  • Reversed polarity: Check LEDs, capacitors, diodes orientation
  • Short circuits: Look for bare wires touching each other

COMMON SOFTWARE PROBLEMS:
  • Wrong pin number: Verify your code pin numbers match wiring
  • Missing pinMode(): Pins default to INPUT — forgot to set OUTPUT
  • Integer division: 5/9 = 0 in C! Use 5.0/9.0 for float division
  • Serial not initialized: Forgot Serial.begin() in setup()
  • Variable scope: Variable declared inside if-block isn't accessible outside

SERIAL MONITOR DEBUGGING:
  The Serial Monitor is your window into what the Arduino is thinking.
  Add Serial.println() statements at key points:

  void setup() {

    digitalWrite(yellowLED, LOW);
    digitalWrite(redLED, LOW);

DAY 22: BUILD THE CHASSIS — MECHANICAL ASSEMBLY
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

🎯 LEARNING OBJECTIVES:
  • Assemble the robot chassis and mount motors
  • Understand mechanical considerations for mobile robots
  • Secure the wheels and test manual rolling
  • Plan component placement

📖 THEORY:

CHASSIS DESIGN BASICS:
  A 2WD (two-wheel drive) robot uses:
  • 2 powered DC motors with wheels (for driving)
  • 1 caster wheel or ball caster (for balance — like a shopping cart wheel)

  The two powered wheels provide both movement AND steering:
  • Both wheels forward → robot goes straight
  • Left wheel only → robot turns right
  • Right wheel only → robot turns left
  • Wheels in opposite directions → robot spins in place

WEIGHT DISTRIBUTION:
  • Keep the heavy components (battery, motors) LOW for stability
  • Center of gravity should be between the drive wheels
  • The sensor should be at the FRONT (to detect obstacles ahead)
  • Arduino and motor driver should be accessible for debugging

🔨 PRACTICAL: CHASSIS ASSEMBLY

Step-by-step:
  1. Lay out all chassis parts and identify them
  2. Mount the two DC motors to the chassis using the included brackets

DAY 23: MOTOR CONTROL SYSTEM — WIRING EVERYTHING UP
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

🎯 LEARNING OBJECTIVES:
  • Mount and wire BOTH motors through the L298N driver
  • Connect Arduino to the motor driver
  • Test both motors independently
  • Verify direction control for each motor

📖 THEORY:

TWO-MOTOR CONTROL:
  The L298N can control TWO motors independently:
  • Motor A: IN1, IN2, ENA → OUT1, OUT2
  • Motor B: IN3, IN4, ENB → OUT3, OUT4

  This is essential because to steer, we need independent control of
  each wheel.

WIRING DIAGRAM:
  L298N          Arduino          Notes
  ─────          ───────          ─────
  IN1         →  pin 5            Motor A direction
  IN2         →  pin 4            Motor A direction
  ENA         →  pin 6 (~PWM)     Motor A speed
  IN3         →  pin 8            Motor B direction
  IN4         →  pin 7            Motor B direction
  ENB         →  pin 3 (~PWM)     Motor B speed
  GND         →  GND              MUST connect!
  +12V        ←  Battery +        Motor power (6V)
  GND         ←  Battery −        Motor power ground

  Motor A wires → OUT1, OUT2 (left wheel)
  Motor B wires → OUT3, OUT4 (right wheel)

🔨 PRACTICAL: DUAL MOTOR TEST

Code:
  // Motor A (Left)
  const int in1 = 5, in2 = 4, enA = 6;
  // Motor B (Right)
  const int in3 = 8, in4 = 7, enB = 3;

  void setup() {
    pinMode(in1, OUTPUT); pinMode(in2, OUTPUT); pinMode(enA, OUTPUT);
    pinMode(in3, OUTPUT); pinMode(in4, OUTPUT); pinMode(enB, OUTPUT);
    Serial.begin(9600);
  }

  void motorA(int speed, bool forward) {
    digitalWrite(in1, forward ? HIGH : LOW);
    digitalWrite(in2, forward ? LOW : HIGH);
    analogWrite(enA, abs(speed));
  }

  void motorB(int speed, bool forward) {
    digitalWrite(in3, forward ? HIGH : LOW);
    digitalWrite(in4, forward ? LOW : HIGH);
    analogWrite(enB, abs(speed));
  }

  void stopAll() {
    analogWrite(enA, 0);
    analogWrite(enB, 0);
  }

  void loop() {
    Serial.println("Motor A Forward");
    motorA(200, true);
    delay(2000);
    stopAll();
    delay(500);

    Serial.println("Motor B Forward");
    motorB(200, true);
    delay(2000);
    stopAll();
    delay(500);

    Serial.println("Both Forward");
    motorA(200, true);
    motorB(200, true);
    delay(2000);
    stopAll();
    delay(2000);
  }

DAY 24: MOVEMENT PROGRAMMING — FORWARD, BACKWARD, TURN
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

🎯 LEARNING OBJECTIVES:
  • Create reusable movement functions
  • Program forward, backward, left turn, and right turn
  • Test each movement with the robot on the ground
  • Fine-tune motor speeds for straight-line driving

📖 THEORY:

DIFFERENTIAL DRIVE STEERING:
  With two wheels, steering works by varying wheel speeds:
  • Forward: Both wheels same speed, same direction
  • Backward: Both wheels same speed, reverse direction
  • Turn left: Right wheel forward, left wheel stopped (or slower)
  • Turn right: Left wheel forward, right wheel stopped (or slower)
  • Spin left: Right wheel forward, left wheel backward
  • Spin right: Left wheel forward, right wheel backward

MOTOR SPEED CALIBRATION:
  No two motors are identical. Even with the same PWM value, one motor
  might spin slightly faster than the other, causing the robot to drift.
  You'll need to calibrate by reducing the faster motor's PWM value.

🔨 PRACTICAL: MOVEMENT FUNCTION LIBRARY

Code:
  const int in1 = 5, in2 = 4, enA = 6;  // Left motor
  const int in3 = 8, in4 = 7, enB = 3;  // Right motor

  // Calibration offsets (adjust these!)
  const int leftOffset = 0;    // Reduce if left motor is faster
  const int rightOffset = 0;   // Reduce if right motor is faster

  void setup() {
    pinMode(in1, OUTPUT); pinMode(in2, OUTPUT); pinMode(enA, OUTPUT);
    pinMode(in3, OUTPUT); pinMode(in4, OUTPUT); pinMode(enB, OUTPUT);
    Serial.begin(9600);
    delay(3000);  // 3-second delay to place robot on ground
    Serial.println("Starting movement test...");
  }

  void moveForward(int speed) {
    digitalWrite(in1, HIGH); digitalWrite(in2, LOW);
    analogWrite(enA, speed - leftOffset);
    digitalWrite(in3, HIGH); digitalWrite(in4, LOW);
    analogWrite(enB, speed - rightOffset);
  }

  void moveBackward(int speed) {
    digitalWrite(in1, LOW); digitalWrite(in2, HIGH);
    analogWrite(enA, speed - leftOffset);
    digitalWrite(in3, LOW); digitalWrite(in4, HIGH);
    analogWrite(enB, speed - rightOffset);
  }

  void turnLeft(int speed) {
    analogWrite(enA, 0);                          // Left wheel stops
    digitalWrite(in3, HIGH); digitalWrite(in4, LOW);
    analogWrite(enB, speed);                      // Right wheel forward
  }

  void turnRight(int speed) {
    digitalWrite(in1, HIGH); digitalWrite(in2, LOW);
    analogWrite(enA, speed);                      // Left wheel forward
    analogWrite(enB, 0);                          // Right wheel stops
  }

  void spinLeft(int speed) {
    digitalWrite(in1, LOW); digitalWrite(in2, HIGH);  // Left backward
    analogWrite(enA, speed);
    digitalWrite(in3, HIGH); digitalWrite(in4, LOW);  // Right forward
    analogWrite(enB, speed);
  }

  void stopRobot() {
    analogWrite(enA, 0);
    analogWrite(enB, 0);
  }

  void loop() {
    Serial.println("Forward");
    moveForward(180);
    delay(2000);

    stopRobot();
    delay(500);

    Serial.println("Turn Right");
    turnRight(180);
    delay(800);

    stopRobot();
    delay(500);

    Serial.println("Forward");
    moveForward(180);
    delay(2000);

    stopRobot();
    delay(500);

    Serial.println("Turn Left");
    turnLeft(180);
    delay(800);

    stopRobot();
    delay(500);

    Serial.println("Backward");
    moveBackward(150);
    delay(1000);

    stopRobot();
    delay(3000);  // Pause before repeating
  }

DAY 26: OBSTACLE AVOIDANCE LOGIC
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

🎯 LEARNING OBJECTIVES:
  • Implement full obstacle avoidance behavior
  • Program the sense-think-act loop for autonomous navigation
  • Handle edge cases (corners, dead ends)
  • Create a truly autonomous robot

📖 THEORY:

OBSTACLE AVOIDANCE ALGORITHM:
  The basic algorithm is simple but effective:

  LOOP FOREVER:
    1. Read distance ahead
    2. IF distance > safe threshold:
         Drive forward
    3. ELSE:
         Stop
         Look left (or just turn)
         IF left is clear:
           Turn left
         ELSE:
           Turn right
         Resume driving

  With a single front-facing sensor, we use a simpler version:
    IF obstacle detected:
       Stop
       Back up slightly
       Turn a random direction
    ELSE:
       Drive forward

PSEUDOCODE:
  IF obstacle_ahead:
     stop
     backup(500ms)
     turn_right(600ms)    // Or random direction
  ELSE:
     move_forward

🔨 PRACTICAL: AUTONOMOUS OBSTACLE AVOIDANCE

DAY 28: POWER OPTIMIZATION — MAKING IT LAST
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

🎯 LEARNING OBJECTIVES:
  • Understand battery capacity and runtime calculations
  • Learn about voltage regulators and power distribution
  • Optimize wiring for reliability
  • Improve overall robot construction quality

📖 THEORY:

BATTERY BASICS FOR ROBOTS:
  Battery capacity is measured in milliamp-hours (mAh).
  • A 2000mAh battery can supply 2000mA for 1 hour, or 500mA for 4 hours

  Runtime estimate:
  Runtime (hours) = Battery capacity (mAh) / Total current draw (mA)

  Typical current draws in your robot:
  • Arduino: ~50mA
  • Ultrasonic sensor: ~15mA
  • Motor driver (L298N): ~36mA (idle)
  • Each motor (running): ~200-400mA
  • Total while driving: ~500-900mA

  With 4xAA batteries (total ~2000mAh at 6V):
  Runtime ≈ 2000 / 700 ≈ 2.8 hours (rough estimate)

  With a 9V battery (~500mAh):
  Runtime ≈ 500 / 700 ≈ 0.7 hours = ~40 minutes
  → 9V batteries are NOT ideal for robots with motors!

POWER SUPPLY OPTIONS (BEST TO WORST):
  1. Li-Po battery (7.4V, 2200mAh) — Best but requires special charger
  2. 4xAA rechargeable NiMH (4.8V, 2000mAh) — Good, affordable
  3. 6xAA alkaline (9V, 2000mAh) — Decent but heavy
  4. 9V battery (9V, 500mAh) — Poor, drains fast

WIRING OPTIMIZATION:
  • Solder connections where possible (more reliable than breadboard)
  • Use heat-shrink tubing on exposed connections
  • Zip-tie wire bundles to the chassis to prevent snagging
  • Label wires if possible (colored tape works great)
  • Keep motor wires short and twisted together
  • Ensure all ground connections are solid

VOLTAGE MONITORING:
  You can monitor battery voltage with the Arduino:

  // Connect battery + through a voltage divider to A1
  // R1 = 10kΩ (battery to A1), R2 = 10kΩ (A1 to GND)
  // This halves the voltage so Arduino can safely read it

  float getBatteryVoltage() {
    int raw = analogRead(A1);
    float voltage = raw * (5.0 / 1023.0) * 2.0; // ×2 for divider
    return voltage;
  }

  // In loop():
  float battery = getBatteryVoltage();
  if (battery < 4.5) {
    Serial.println("LOW BATTERY WARNING!");
    // Flash an LED, slow down, or stop
  }

LEVEL 2 — INTERMEDIATE PROJECTS:
  1. LINE-FOLLOWING ROBOT
     • Add IR line sensors to follow a black line on white surface
     • Learn PID control for smooth line tracking

  2. PID CONTROL (Proportional-Integral-Derivative)
     • Advanced algorithm for precise motor control
     • Makes your robot follow lines perfectly and drive straight
     • Formula: output = Kp×error + Ki×∫error + Kd×(Δerror/Δt)

  3. REMOTE CONTROL
     • Add Bluetooth module (HC-05/HC-06)
     • Control your robot from your phone
     • Build a custom app with MIT App Inventor

  4. MORE SENSORS
     • IR obstacle sensors (digital, faster than ultrasonic)
     • Temperature/humidity sensors (DHT11/DHT22)
     • Light sensors (LDR — photoresistor)
     • IMU/accelerometer (MPU6050)

LEVEL 3 — ADVANCED:
  1. RASPBERRY PI
     • Full Linux computer on a board
     • Camera vision, WiFi, machine learning
     • Program in Python

  2. ROS (Robot Operating System)
     • Industry-standard robotics framework
     • Simulation, mapping, path planning
     • Used in research and commercial robots

  3. 3D PRINTING
     • Design and print custom robot parts
     • Use Fusion 360 or TinkerCAD for design

  4. COMPUTER VISION
     • Camera + OpenCV for object detection
     • Face tracking, color tracking
     • QR code reading

  5. ROBOTIC ARM
     • Servo motors for precise positioning
     • Inverse kinematics
     • Pick-and-place tasks

⚠️ HONEST ADVICE FROM THE TRENCHES
================================================================================

Things you WILL struggle with:
  • Wiring mistakes — you'll spend 30 minutes only to find one loose wire
  • Code bugs — typos, wrong pin numbers, missing semicolons
  • Motors not working — power issues, driver connections, dead batteries
  • Sensors giving garbage data — noise, bad connections, wrong code
  • Robot going in circles — motor calibration, wheel alignment
  • Components burning out — wrong voltage, wrong polarity, no protection

THAT'S NOT A PROBLEM — THAT'S THE TRAINING.

Every professional robotics engineer has burned out dozens of LEDs, fried
a few Arduinos, and spent hours debugging a wire that was one row off on
the breadboard. The difference between a beginner and an expert isn't that
experts don't make mistakes — it's that experts know how to find and fix
them quickly.

Keep building. Keep breaking things. Keep learning.

Welcome to robotics. 🤖
